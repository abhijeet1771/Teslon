# Teslon Must Earn Every Skip Before Launch

**Do not launch Teslon with the promise "never a wrong skip, never silent". The evidence does not support it today.** The product idea is right, and nobody else offers it. No commercial or open-source tool gives testers a per-area, machine-checked "safe to skip" from a static reading of two commits. The problem is also real: when developers pick regression tests by hand, they choose unsafely in about a quarter of sessions. But when Teslon was run on code it had not been tuned on, it broke its own promise many times. In 60 adversarial pull requests, the red team got **4 wrong skips, each certified by the independent checker and shown at "High" confidence, plus 15 silent misses**. Each was a one-line change a developer could plausibly make. On real open-source apps, Teslon knew **0 of 33 screens and 0 of 17 endpoints** in spring-boot-admin. It called a four-major-version Python crypto upgrade **"nothing to test, High confidence"**. It crashed on **all 21 commits** of halo, and on n8n it gave no answer within 20 minutes. It is also **not enterprise grade**. Its cost grows with the size of the repository, not the size of the PR. It writes secret values into report.html, although SECURITY.md says it never does. And the GitHub Action downloads and compiles Teslon from npm on every run. "100% without mistakes" cannot be reached in principle (it is a theorem), and it cannot be shown by testing either. What Teslon *can* honestly reach is a one-sided, printed guarantee: no wrong skip, under stated assumptions, with a measured upper bound on the error rate. **The recommendation is a staged launch.** First, after about 6-9 weeks of fail-closed and enterprise-floor fixes, an advisory beta in shadow mode with design partners, making no numeric claim. Then, after compiler-backed facts, a decoupled checker and at least **299 independently checked real PRs per change-kind stratum with zero wrong skips** (wrong-skip rate below 1% at 95% confidence), a general launch with a numeric claim. That is about 6-9 months away.

> **About the evidence.** The strongest evidence here was produced by running Teslon itself (red team, engineering audit, field tests, soundness experiments). Every measured finding links to its research note and gives the command that reproduces it. The external literature and market claims had to come mostly from search-result summaries, because the session's network blocked scholarly and vendor hosts. Those claims keep their tags: **[S]** = seen in a search-result summary of the source, **[Sec]** = secondary source, **[Mem]** = the researcher's memory, not re-checked. **None of the following was checked against the full primary text:** the 27% unsafe manual selection, the "half of changes untested, five times more defects" figure, GPT-5's median F1 of 0.0, Meta's 99.9% and Shopify's 99.94%, the call-graph recall figures (0.884 and 61%), the CI market shares, the regulatory clause numbers and dates, and every vendor price. The Clopper-Pearson bounds were computed for this report and are exact. One field test, `field_readable_stack.md`, **was stopped early**. Its reference lists, reader coverage and jhipster-registry results are complete, but the spring-boot-admin mass run, hand-truth and history steps and its final verdict were never finished. No ThingsBoard scale test was run; scale evidence comes from the engineering audit's n8n and train-ticket runs.

## The idea is right, the field is empty, and the shortcuts are wrong

**What Teslon gets right is substantial, and the evidence backs it.** Testers really do scope regression by experience. A 450-session field study found developers' hand-picked selections differed from automated selection in every session and missed needed tests in 27% of them ([Gligoric et al. 2014](https://users.ece.utexas.edu/~gligoric/papers/GligoricETAL14ManualRTS.pdf) [S]). A 14-month industrial study reported that about half of all changes reached production untested, and that untested changes carried up to five times more defects. That finding comes from a single 340k-line C# system ([Haas et al. 2025, summarising Eder et al. 2013](https://www.se.cs.uni-saarland.de/publications/docs/HSJ+25.pdf) [Sec]). The commercial survey found **no product that gives a per-item, machine-checked skip at screen, API or job level from static reading.** Every skip in the market is relative to recorded coverage (Datadog, SeaLights, Teamscale, Parasoft), is statistical (Develocity, Launchable), or is an LLM's judgement that falls back to running everything when unsure ([Momentic AI Select docs](https://momentic.ai/docs/ai/select)), as the [commercial landscape note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/commercial_landscape.md>) shows.

Keeping LLMs out of every decision is supported by the only controlled measurement found. There, GPT-5 scored a median precision, recall and F1 of **0.0** on manually labelled code-change impact ([Stengg et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19481) [S]; the figure comes from a search summary, not the full paper). Other parts of the build also earn credit. The analysis is deterministic: two runs differ only in the run-time field. Output escaping survived hostile PR titles and file names. Every process launch uses argument arrays, so there is no shell. CI tests the product's promise and not just its code. And the 434 compiler-proven mutants plus 32 super-hard PRs ended fully trusted after the fixes they forced ([engineering audit](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/engineering_audit.md>) §6; [GAUNTLET](</home/user/teslon/research/GAUNTLET-2026-11-05.md>)). All 55 live GitHub PR comments matched the lab answer ([LIVE-RUN](</home/user/teslon/research/LIVE-RUN-2026-11-05.md>)). Most important of all, the design rule "hold as unclear when unsure" works wherever it is actually applied. Across 20 commits each of eShop, saleor-dashboard and train-ticket, Teslon produced **no skip at all from a stack it does not read** ([unread-stack field test](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/field_unread_stacks.md>) §5).

**What Teslon gets wrong is narrower, but more dangerous than its own documents admit.** The key synthesis of this research is that **Teslon's usefulness and its danger come from the same shortcuts.** On a real app, the only way Teslon produces a crisp, low-effort answer is to decide a file "has no effect". It does that through rules that guess instead of check: file-name and folder rules, line-prefix filters, `git diff -b`, and token comparisons that leave tokens out. Those are exactly the places where it was wrong. A Java class named `Security.java`, a Vue component named `Notice.vue`, a `license.ts` service and a `requirements.txt` are all classed as "documentation" (`kinds.ts:13-15`). A front-end folder named `charts/` is classed as "deployment" (`kinds.ts:21`). Any changed line starting with `import`, `*` or `/*` is treated as "not code" (`impact.ts:571`, `where.ts:54`). Method modifiers are not part of a method's identity (`java.ts:42-50`). The second structural mistake is in what the "independent checker" means. **It re-checks only skips** (`impact.ts:694-697`), so every "no effect" verdict goes unchecked. And it reads the same file kinds, the same Java facts and the same setting-reader model as the engine. In plain terms, it is a second accountant re-adding the same receipts: when a receipt is missing, both totals agree and both are wrong ([red team](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/redteam_soundness.md>) §2).

Four further misjudgements follow from these two. First, the promise is stated as absolute while all the evidence was written by the people who wrote the rules. Second, the change atlas marks rows "precise" that the red team contradicted: config overrides, deployment descriptors, and "no effect" calls on trivial-looking edits ([failure empirics note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/change_failure_empirics_postmortems.md>) §7). Third, the rules were shaped by fixture cases, and nearby variants break them. The deploy-profile rule handles only `SPRING_PROFILES_ACTIVE`; the "served folder" list happens to cover OrderDesk's `webapp/` but not a typical Vite `frontend/src/` ([red team](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/redteam_soundness.md>) §5). Fourth, the report serves one persona, the manual tester, and gives automation engineers nothing to run ([QA voice note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/qa_practitioner_voice_report_design.md>) §6).

## A 100% guarantee is impossible, but a bounded, printed one is not

**"Will it be 100% without mistakes?" No, for three independent reasons.** First, deciding whether a commit changes what an area does is a non-trivial semantic property of programs, so Rice's theorem makes it undecidable. Any tool that always terminates must err somewhere: either a missed impact or a needless test. Second, every serious whole-program analyser for Java or JavaScript deliberately leaves some features out, and the field's own norm is to document which ones ([Livshits et al., "In Defense of Soundiness", CACM 2015](https://cacm.acm.org/magazines/2015/2/182650-in-defense-of-soundiness/fulltext) [S]). Third, no amount of testing can show zero errors. If 0 wrong skips are seen in *n* independent decisions, that bounds the true rate only below about 3/*n* at 95% confidence ([Hanley and Lippman-Hand, JAMA 1983](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/389714)), and ultra-high reliability needs "exorbitant amounts of testing" ([Butler and Finelli 1993](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040139817/downloads/20040139817.pdf) [S]). Today's 55 live PRs, all on Teslon's own fixtures, bound the wrong-skip rate only below about 5.3%, and only for PRs like those. The 120 historical commits checked citations and crashes, not whether the skips were right.

What *is* achievable is a **one-sided, conditional guarantee**, the same shape as every tool that claims soundness. The static analyser Astrée is sound only under hypotheses that "must be validated by some other means" ([Kästner et al. ERTS 2010](https://www.di.ens.fr/~rival/papers/erts10.pdf) [S]). Safe test selection is defined as "under certain well-defined conditions" ([Rothermel and Harrold 1996](https://core.ac.uk/download/17237569.pdf) [S]). Industry selectors openly accept small miss budgets because a later full run catches what they miss. Meta still reports over 99.9% of faulty changes ([Machalica et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05286) [S]), and Shopify's static selector caught 99.94% of failures, with its remaining misses root-caused one by one ([Shopify Engineering](https://shopify.engineering/spark-joy-by-running-fewer-tests) [S]). **A manual-QA skip has no later full run behind it, so Teslon must hold itself to a stricter bar than any of these. Until it does, it should tell teams to keep their nightly full run.**

> "Teslon reads Java/Spring services with Angular or Vue 3 front ends (Maven, npm, Liquibase, Flyway, YAML), offline and with no AI in any decision. It re-reads every line it cites at the head commit. A 'skip' means Teslon's own readers found no path from this pull request's changed code to that area, and a second walk over the same facts agreed, under the assumptions printed with it. That is advice, not proof. It has not yet been measured on real teams' code, and our own adversarial tests found wrong skips and missed changes, which we publish with their fixes. So keep your regular regression run."

**The sentence Teslon can state after the roadmap below** (with N and the bound filled in per stratum from the pilots):

> "For [listed stacks and change kinds], a 'skip' means the compiled code of both commits shows no path from the change to that area under your deployed profiles. That is re-derived by a checker that shares no reader with the engine, and every assumption is printed with the skip. Anything Teslon cannot read or prove is listed as 'needs a check', never left out. Across [N ≥ 299] independently checked real pull requests in this category, none of its skips was wrong, which bounds the wrong-skip rate below [1%] at 95% confidence."

**The evidence bar for launch.** The figures below are exact Clopper-Pearson bounds, computed here. If 0 wrong skips are seen in *n* independent checked decisions, the one-sided 95% upper bound falls below **1% at n = 299** and below **0.1% at n = 2,995**. If one wrong skip has already been found, the counts rise to **473** and **4,742**.

A "checked decision" is one PR's set of skips, judged correct only if *every* skip in it holds. That is the unit a test lead acts on, and it avoids counting correlated skips from the same PR as independent. Each decision is judged three ways. The truth is written before Teslon's answer is read; per-request coverage is recorded on the skipped areas; and any escaped defect a pilot's QA later finds is traced back to the skip.

Flaky results must be filtered, because flaky transitions are known to distort test-selection evaluations ([Leong et al. 2019](https://mpapad.github.io/publications/pdfs/ICSE-SEIP2019.pdf) [S]). Raw SZZ (tracing bugs back to the commits that introduced them) cannot be used unvalidated, because about half the "bug-fixing" commits it finds are not bug fixes ([Herbold et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1911.08938) [S]).

Evidence is counted per **stratum**: back-end Java code, front-end Angular/Vue code, configuration and deployment files, dependencies and build files, and data and migrations. That makes about 1,500 checked PR decisions in total, drawn from at least 5 repositories per stratum, with no repository supplying more than 20% of a stratum. A stratum that misses the bar ships with today's sentence, not the stronger one.

Three numbers are reported alongside, never one alone: the wrong-skip bound, the share of areas held as "needs a check" (abstention), and the share listed to test that were not affected (extra tests). A tool that holds everything is trivially safe and useless. On jhipster-registry today, every screen was unclear in 84% of commits.

The proposed usefulness target is at least one sound skip on 30% of supported-stack PRs, with no more than 20% of areas held. That is a proposal for the team to calibrate in the beta, not a sourced norm. The method copies the closest published template: replay the selector over real historical failures from industrial partners (Haas et al. used 2,622 failures across five subjects, [TSE 2024](https://teamscale.com/hubfs/Publications/2024-optimization-of-automated-and-manual-software-tests.pdf) [S]). It also compares Teslon's miss rate with what the same teams' testers chose unaided. The only field study found has developers' manual selection missing needed tests in 27% of sessions ([Gligoric et al. 2014](https://users.ece.utexas.edu/~gligoric/papers/GligoricETAL14ManualRTS.pdf) [S]). "Measurably safer than how your team scopes today" is a claim Teslon can earn early.

## Becoming one of one means inventing the proof, not adding rules

**What must be built into Teslon.** The theme is to stop re-implementing compilers and frameworks with rules, and to read what they emit instead ([soundness note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/soundness_theory_framework_modelling.md>) Q3-Q8; [open-source note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/open_source_landscape.md>) Q12). The pieces are these:

- **Compiler-grade facts.** Use [scip-java](https://github.com/sourcegraph/scip-java) (Apache-2.0) for Java and Kotlin symbols. Use the TypeScript TypeChecker Teslon already ships for React, Next.js, Express and NestJS. Use vue-tsc for Vue templates and Angular's own template type-checker for Angular.
- **A compiler-identity check for "nothing changed".** Compiling both versions and comparing the output proves "same" soundly and cheaply. It does not recognise every equivalent-but-different edit (about 30% of equivalent mutants in the original study), but it never lets a real change through ([Papadakis et al. ICSE 2015](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/20289/1/ICSE15.pdf) [S]).
- **Spring facts from the framework.** Take bean definitions from Spring AOT, which evaluates `@Profile` and `@Conditional` at build time ([Spring Framework AOT](https://docs.spring.io/spring-framework/reference/7.0/core/aot.html) [S]), or from the Spring team's own [Spring Tools metamodel index](https://github.com/spring-projects/spring-tools). The Tools index is EPL, so it must run as a separate process.
- **Build-derived file classification.** Decide what is code from what the build actually uses: package.json `files`, Maven resource roots, Dockerfile `COPY`, and knip's catalogue of framework entry points. Never decide it from a name.
- **A blob-hash incremental cache**, the same idea as bazel-diff and Nx input hashing.
- **A closed catalogue of change kinds proven harmless.** The strongest published pattern for skipping with proof is a catalogue of behaviour-preserving change kinds, each backed by tests ([Liu et al. ISSTA 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10467385-more-precise-regression-test-selection-via-reasoning-about-semantics-modifying-changes) [S]). Reachability alone is not enough.
- **A co-change veto.** A deterministic check of files that usually change together turns "this PR changed only half the pair" into "needs a check". Microsoft deployed this approach for 14 months across 360 repositories ([Rex, NSDI 2020](https://usenix.org/system/files/nsdi20-paper-mehta.pdf) [S]).
- **Config keys as first-class graph nodes, per profile.** Research found that code-only selectors are unsafe for configuration changes by construction ([uRTS, ICSE 2023](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2023/icse-2023-technical-track/59/Test-Selection-for-Unified-Regression-Testing) [S]).
- **API-contract diffs** with [oasdiff](https://github.com/oasdiff/oasdiff), and a banner on every changed endpoint saying that consumers outside this repository are not checked.
- **Mapping to existing tests**, so automation engineers get "run these, no test reaches those".
- **New change kinds where the failure data is heaviest.** Error-handling code caused 92% of catastrophic failures in one study of distributed systems ([Yuan et al. OSDI 2014](https://www.eecg.utoronto.ca/~stumm/Papers/Yuan-OSDI-14.pdf) [Sec]). Code that consumes runtime data was the latent defect behind the Google Cloud June 2025 outage ([GCP report](https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents/ow5i3PPK96RduMcb1SsW) [Sec]) and the Cloudflare November 2025 outage ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/18-november-2025-outage/) [Sec]). Feature flags, mixed-version rollouts and hard-coded limits belong in the same list. Each can be a "needs a check" with a concrete check, so adding them cannot break the no-wrong-skip promise.

Licences constrain several of these choices. CodeQL's command-line terms forbid use on code that is not open source ([CodeQL CLI terms](https://github.com/github/codeql-cli-binaries)). Liquibase is under the Functional Source License ([repo](https://github.com/liquibase/liquibase)). OpenRewrite's JavaScript, Python, C# and Go modules carry a source-available licence that blocks products built on them. So these can serve only as lab oracles or checklists, never as embedded parts.

**What must be invented, because the searches found it nowhere.** These are inferences from absence across the literature, commercial and open-source surveys, so treat them as "not found", not "proven not to exist".

1. **A public, screen/API/job-level PR-impact benchmark with ground truth.** The largest public impact benchmark is Java method-level co-change, and no dataset maps code changes to affected screens ([academic note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/academic_cia_rts.md>) Q3, Q6). Publishing one, with per-tool wrong-skip bounds, would make Teslon the reference point for the whole category.
2. **A portable skip certificate that a third party can check.** The idea is a small, separately written kernel that re-derives each skip from compiler and framework outputs. This follows the de Bruijn principle: trust comes from a checker small enough to audit, not from the producer. Today's certificate is a digest of Teslon's own facts.
3. **Soundness telemetry on the customer's own runs.** Every customer run would report its reader coverage (Teslon's inventory against the build's inventory) and its trace contradictions (skips contradicted by the team's own test runs). Every run then becomes evidence, and the report can print "skips contradicted on this repo: 0 of N".
4. **Measured recall for framework-aware call graphs on Spring and on Angular/Vue.** The soundness researcher found no published recall figure for TypeScript front-end frameworks, and no Spring-aware recall measured against a dynamic baseline on real Spring Boot services.
5. **A qualification kit for a change-impact tool.** ISO 26262 and DO-330 class a tool that can fail to flag an affected item as one needing qualification ([Synopsys on ISO 26262 tool confidence](https://www.synopsys.com/content/dam/synopsys/solutions/documents/automotive-tool-confidence-iso-26262-wp.pdf) [S]; DO-330 criteria [Mem]). No such kit for change-impact analysis was found.

**What the owner is likely forgetting.** These are grouped by the areas the owner named.

**Pilots.** The beta needs written design-partner agreements: shadow mode, truth-first adjudication, access to past QA escapes, and permission to publish anonymised numbers. It should include one tester, one automation engineer, one lead and one manager per partner, because the persona specification below has never been tested with users.

**Validation.** Use independent red-teamers, keep the rule authors away from the truth labels, filter flaky results, and validate SZZ by hand.

**Liability.** MIT's "AS IS" covers free use. Enterprise contracts will want a limited warranty (the product works as documented), a liability cap, and a clause that the customer owns each test decision ([enterprise note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/enterprise_readiness_compliance.md>) §6 [Mem]). The product wording should match: "out of regression scope for this PR (proof)" plus "keep your nightly full run".

**Licensing.** Today's MIT licence lets any competitor fork and resell Teslon, and contributions under "Teslon contributors" with no CLA cannot later be relicensed. Decide between open core, a source-available licence and proprietary before the first external contribution.

**Pricing.** Buyers anchor on $8-20 per committer per month for test-impact tools and coverage ([Datadog pricing](https://datadoghq.com/pricing); third-party figures in the [commercial note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/commercial_landscape.md>) §10) and about $24-40 per developer for AI reviewers ([techplained 2026](https://www.techplained.com/best-ai-code-review-tools) [Sec]). The QA budget holder is not the committer population, so a hybrid fits: free for open source, per committer for private repositories, plus QA seats for test-management features. Above about $20 per committer, Teslon needs the measured accuracy story.

**Release.** Signed, tagged releases, an SBOM, a vulnerability disclosure policy with an SLA, and an air-gapped bundle.

**Integrations.** GitHub Actions is the main CI in only about 41% of organisations; GitLab CI is at 34% and Jenkins at 31% ([JetBrains State of CI/CD 2025](https://blog.jetbrains.com/teamcity/2025/10/the-state-of-cicd/) [S]). So output formats come before vendor plugins. For test leads, the integration that matters is importing the TEST list into Xray or TestRail as a test run, with the SKIP reasons recorded.

**Regulated buyers.** Banking and payments come first. PCI DSS 6.5.1 asks for each change's documented impact, approval and testing ([PCI 6.5.1](https://withpci.pages.dev/requirements/6/6.5/6.5.1) [S]). Those buyers need change-request, requirement and test-case IDs, a sign-off block, and retention well beyond the Action's 30-day artifact. IEC 61508-3 describes impact analysis as deciding "which modules are impacted, which activities must be repeated", which matches Teslon's design almost word for word ([exida](https://www.exida.com/Blog/so_whats_an_impact_analysis) [S]).

**Accessibility.** A WCAG 2.2 AA audit of report.html and an Accessibility Conformance Report for public-sector deals.

**Competition.** The most dangerous competitor is a bundle, not a startup. Tricentis now owns SeaLights, LiveCompare and qTest, and bought Tabnine's code-context engine in July 2026 ([BusinessWire](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260730388427/en/Tricentis-Acquires-Tabnine-to-Further-Scale-Agentic-Quality-Engineering-for-the-Enterprise) [S]). CodeRabbit now markets "blast radius" triage to developers ([CodeRabbit](https://coderabbit.ai/blog/introducing-agentic-change-management) [S]). Against both, Teslon's defensible edge is determinism, working offline, and published accuracy, not the idea itself.

**Maintainability.** The engine has no reader interface, and individual functions run to 1,000 lines or more. Adding React or Node today means editing those functions, which have no direct unit tests ([engineering audit](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/engineering_audit.md>) H2, H6).

## A report each QA role can act on in its own unit

**Today the shipped PR comment serves the manual tester well and the other three roles poorly.** The research team rendered the real comment and summary for spring-petclinic-microservices PR #559 (`dc9ca15..1d76b00`) with the built engine. The driver is `/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-Orbis/a99ca81c-9750-554d-8faa-4222ad38b407/scratchpad/field/qavoice/render.mjs`, and the outputs are in the same folder. The comment gets the first ten seconds right: three counts, page › section › field steps written as Do → Expect → Before, and file:line proof re-checked 71 of 71 times.

But it gives automation engineers no test to run. It prints "High confidence" next to its own unseen items. It marks 16 of 20 cases as "first", which is not a ranking. It labels hedged outcomes "(Proven)". It uses ✅, the universal "passed" mark, for "skip". And it counts monitoring and DBA tasks as tests ([QA voice note](<../research_notes/Teslon launch readiness research/qa_practitioner_voice_report_design.md>) §6, gaps G1-G18).

The evidence on trust says why this matters. Users over-rely on automation that looks authoritative and then abandon it after a visible miss. Explanations help only when they are specific and checkable. And developers' distrust of AI accuracy rose from 31% to 46% in one year ([Stack Overflow 2025 via TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-developer-trust-in-ai-declines/) [S]).

**The specification has one shared verdict, four first views, and every number one click from its proof.**

The vocabulary should follow ISTQB, the standard most enterprise QA staff are certified in. Under it, "confirmation testing" checks the intended change and "regression testing" checks for side effects ([imbus ISTQB glossary](https://www.imbus.de/en/glossar/term/confirmation-testing) [S]). A *test condition* is counted once, not once per entry point (UI, API, chat). "Safe to skip" becomes "**not affected — out of regression scope for this PR (proof)**". "Unclear" becomes "**needs a check**", with a check that can actually be done and an owner. "Confidence" becomes "**evidence completeness**", shown next to a separate "**outside Teslon's view**" line. Colour must never carry meaning alone ([WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). The PR comment stays one comment, updated in place and capped in length, because bot noise is the main complaint developers make about bots ([Wessel et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13950) [S]).

**Layer 1 and Layer 2.** Layer 1 is the four role views above, as tabs in the run summary and in the cockpit. Layer 2 is the drill-down: area, then case, then the evidence chain, with plain words first and code identifiers second. An uncertainty appears at the level where it changes a decision, never only in a footer. A hedged outcome reads "Unknown: observe and report", never "Expect".

The manager view needs run history, which Teslon does not keep today. That makes history storage, and the "skips contradicted on this repo" counter, product features in Phase 2-3. They are not polish: they are how Teslon shows its accuracy to the people who decide whether to keep it.

## Conclusion

The research changes the owner's question. The question is no longer "is the engine accurate enough?" but "**whose facts does the proof rest on?**" Every failure that mattered, from the certified wrong skips to the silent "nothing to test" and the empty screen lists on real apps, came from Teslon trusting its own readings, at moments when its own design said to hold. Adding more rules will not close that gap. The fixtures already showed it: each round of rules fixed the case in front of the team and missed the next variant. What closes the gap is to move the proof onto facts produced by compilers, frameworks and the team's own test runs. Then the checker becomes a true second opinion, and each customer run adds evidence instead of risk.

Done that way, Teslon's rarest asset is not the claim "never wrong". It is the ability to publish, per stack and change kind, a measured and bounded wrong-skip rate that no competitor publishes. Today Teslon is safe mostly because it is rarely useful, and useful mostly where it is unsafe. The roadmap reverses that, and the per-persona report turns each verified skip into hours a test lead can defend. The fastest credible path is to fail closed in three weeks, run a shadow beta with real teams within about two months, and make the numeric claim only once the real-PR evidence has earned it.
